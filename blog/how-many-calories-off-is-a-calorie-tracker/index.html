How many calories off is a calorie tracker? 330 to 579

Three doubly labeled water studies put logged intake 330 to 579 calories a day under measured expenditure. The food database was off by 26.

Published October 23, 2026

Om Divyatej, founder of MealSaid

Key takeaways

  • Across three doubly labeled water studies, logged intake came in 330 to 579 calories a day below measured energy expenditure.
  • MyFitnessPal's database landed within 26 calories a day of a national reference database over 50 people's four-day records, so the database is not where the error lives.
  • The group average hides the day. One photo app was 330 calories low on average with 95% limits of agreement from 1,504 under to 845 over.
  • Of 269 restaurant items put through bomb calorimetry, 50 contained at least 100 calories more than the menu said and 141 contained less.
  • A packaged food in the US is only misbranded once it exceeds its stated calories by more than 20%, so a 2,000 calorie day of labels can legally be 2,400.

A calorie tracker's food database is off by about 26 calories a day. What you tell it is off by 330 to 579. Every published measurement I could open says the same thing: the numbers in the app are close, and the gap between what you log and what you actually eat is the whole problem.

I went looking for this because people keep asking me whether a bigger food database would make MealSaid more accurate. The honest answer is no, and the research is not subtle about it. Checked 23 October 2026. Every figure below links to the paper it came from.

How many calories a day is the gap, really?

Between 330 and 579 calories a day, in the three studies that measured real energy expenditure rather than asking people a second time.

Doubly labeled water is the reference method. You drink isotope-labeled water, researchers track how fast the labels leave your body, and that gives total energy expenditure without anyone reporting anything. In weight-stable people it is the closest thing to a true intake number.

Boushey and colleagues dosed 45 adults, had them photograph every eating occasion for 7.5 days, and had trained analysts estimate the calories. Measured expenditure averaged 2,932 calories a day. Reported intake averaged 2,353, "a resulting difference of 579 kcal/day" (Nutrients 2017).

Serra and colleagues ran 30 women through seven days of doubly labeled water in Zurich while they logged with SNAQ, a food-recognition app. The gap was 330 calories a day, or 12.8% low. A one-day 24 hour recall in the same 30 women was 543 calories low, 22.9% (Frontiers in Nutrition 2023).

Foster and colleagues checked Intake24, an online recall tool, against doubly labeled water in 98 UK adults aged 40 to 65. People under-reported by 25% on a first recall and 25% again on the average of three (Journal of Nutritional Science 2019).

Is it the app's database, or is it you?

It is you, by roughly thirteen to one. The database gap is 26 calories a day and the reporting gap is 330.

Evenepoel and colleagues took 50 people's four-day food records and ran them through MyFitnessPal and through Nubel, the Belgian national food composition database. Energy came out at 1,984 calories a day in MyFitnessPal against 1,958 in Nubel, with a correlation of 0.96 (JMIR 2020).

That is a 26 calorie difference, my own subtraction on their Table 1. Put it next to the 330 calorie gap Serra measured and the database accounts for about 8% of the problem.

The same paper found the database is not uniformly fine. Sodium came out at 1,293 mg a day against the reference 2,638, cholesterol at 55 mg against 242, and fiber at 15 g against 19 (Table 1). If you track sodium, that matters a lot; if you track calories, it does not.

Why does a 12.8% average still ruin a week?

Because the average is not the error you get. Serra's 95% limits of agreement ran from 1,504 calories under to 845 calories over (Table 3).

A Bland-Altman limit of agreement says where 95% of individual measurements fall. For the photo app, a given woman's day could plausibly be 1,504 calories low or 845 calories high, and the midpoint of 330 describes nobody in particular.

Foster's numbers say the same thing in percentages. The point estimate was 25% under, and the limits ran from 73% under to 68% over (Foster 2019).

73% under to 68% overthe 95% limits of agreement for one online recall against doubly labeled water, n = 98Foster et al., 2019

Boushey put the same thing in people rather than intervals. Of the 45 adults, 53% were classified as underreporters, and that group was off by a mean of 1,000 calories a day (Boushey 2017).

Are the numbers on the menu any better?

On average yes, per item no. Of 269 restaurant foods burned in a calorimeter, 50 held at least 100 calories more than the menu claimed (Urban 2011).

Urban and colleagues bought food from 42 randomly selected quick-serve and sit-down restaurants in three US states and measured the energy by bomb calorimetry (JAMA 2011). The overall gap was 10 calories a portion, nowhere near significant.

Then the detail: 108 items (40%) were at least 10 calories over the stated value, 141 (52%) were at least 10 calories under, 20 were within 10 either way, and 50 (19%) were at least 100 calories over (JAMA 2011). One side dish was over by 1,000 calories.

Their earlier paper picked the lowest-calorie items on each menu. Those 29 restaurant foods measured 18% above the stated value, and 10 supermarket frozen meals measured 8% above (J Am Diet Assoc 2010).

Packaged food has a legal tolerance, and it is wide. A food is misbranded only if the composite is "greater than 20 percent in excess of the value" declared (21 CFR 101.9(g)(5)). The same rule calls reasonable deficiencies acceptable, so there is no stated floor at all.

Do photo apps fix any of this?

No. The one controlled test I can find had four of them 250 to 345 calories low per meal (NIDDK, 2026).

Researchers at the NIDDK prepared 102 meals in a metabolic kitchen, weighed every ingredient to 0.1 g, photographed them, and ran the photos through MyFitnessPal, Lose It!, Cal AI and Appediet. The apps came in 250 to 345 calories under per meal and about 30 g under on fat (American Society for Nutrition, July 2026).

It is a conference abstract, so treat it as preliminary. I pulled the per-app breakdown apart in the Cal AI accuracy post, where the worst performer was also the best rated.

What do you do with a 330 calorie error?

Use the tracker as a consistent ruler, not as a scale. Set your target from your own logged average and your weight trend, and stop comparing it to a formula.

If you log 1,800 and the truth is 2,100, and you lose weight on that, then 1,800 in your app is your maintenance minus a deficit. The absolute number never needed to be right. What has to be stable is how you log, which is why the spread in those limits of agreement matters more than the bias.

Four things that actually shrink the gap, each one from a study above:

  1. Log the meal as you eat it. Foster's single recall was 25% under, and a recall is what you do when you log at bedtime.
  2. Log the forgotten things first. Boushey's underreporters missed 1,000 calories a day, and the items people photographed outside their provided meals were sugar sweetened beverages, coffee, tea and alcohol.
  3. Distrust the menu before you distrust the app. The calorimeter found 50 of 269 restaurant items over by 100 calories or more.
  4. Keep one logging habit rather than one accurate day. The limits of agreement are wide, so your average over a fortnight carries more information than any single total.

How I checked this

Checked 23 October 2026. I read each paper at PubMed Central and took the numbers from the results text or the tables, not from abstracts alone or from secondary coverage. Sample sizes: 45, 30, 98 and 50 adults for the four validation studies, and 269 food items from 42 restaurants for the calorimetry work.

The 26 calorie database gap and the 13 to 1 ratio are my arithmetic on Evenepoel's Table 1 and Serra's Table 5, not figures either paper prints. The NIDDK meal study is a NUTRITION 2026 conference abstract and has not been peer reviewed, so it sits apart from the rest.

What I could not verify: any controlled comparison of two consumer calorie apps against the same weighed meals with the database held constant, which is the test that would settle the database question properly. Questions

How many calories off is a calorie tracker?

The database is close and the logging is not. In studies that measured real energy expenditure with doubly labeled water, what people logged was 330 to 579 calories a day under what they burned.

Is MyFitnessPal's calorie database accurate?

For energy, yes. Over 50 people's four-day records it gave 1,984 calories a day where a national reference database gave 1,958, a gap of 26 calories. Its sodium and cholesterol values were less than half the reference values.

Why do two apps show different calories for the same food?

Mostly because of the entry you pick and the portion you assume, not the underlying numbers. The published app-versus-reference gap for energy is around 1%, while portion and omission errors run to hundreds of calories.

Does a calorie tracker still work if it is 300 calories off?

Yes, if the error is consistent, because you set your target from your own logged average and your weight trend rather than from a formula. It stops working when the error swings, and the published limits of agreement are wide.

Are photo based calorie apps more accurate than typing?

Not in the one controlled test I can find. Four photo apps run over 102 meals weighed to 0.1 g came in 250 to 345 calories low per meal.
