Cronometer Gold costs $59.99 a year against MacroFactor's $71.99, and Cronometer's free tier tracks every nutrient it has while MacroFactor has none at all. MacroFactor is the only one of the two that reads nutrition back out of Apple Health, which is the thing that matters if you ever log in a second app.

I read both help centres and both US App Store listings on 24 October 2026. Every price and feature below comes off those pages.

I build a calorie tracker myself, so treat the last section as the biased one and the rest as what the documents say.

## Which one costs less in 2026?

**Cronometer Gold at $59.99 a year, against $71.99 for MacroFactor.**

Cronometer's US App Store listing shows two Gold purchases, Gold Monthly at $10.99 and Gold Annual at $59.99 ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/cronometer-calorie-counter/id1145935738)).

MacroFactor's help centre lists $11.99 a month, $47.99 for half a year and $71.99 a year for a single app, and the same three prices appear on its App Store page ([MacroFactor](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/393-how-macrofactor-subscriptions-and-bundles-work)).

There is a fourth MacroFactor price. A bundle covering the separate MacroFactor Workouts app is $89.99 a year, and anyone who subscribed before 1 January 2026 keeps Workouts free until 12 January 2027 ([MacroFactor](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/393-how-macrofactor-subscriptions-and-bundles-work)).

![Three bars of annual price divided by nutrients tracked: MacroFactor 1.29 dollars per nutrient, Cronometer Gold 63 cents, and Cronometer's free Basic account zero for the same 95 nutrients](/blog/img/macrofactor-vs-cronometer-1.webp)

## Is either one actually free?

**Only Cronometer, and its free account is not a stub.**

A Basic account covers all 95 nutrients, the whole database of over a million entries, unlimited barcode scanning, custom meals and recipes, and device syncing ([Cronometer](https://cronometer.com/features/free-nutrition-tracking-app.html)). The limits are the advertising and a seven day window on your nutrition history, with two charts in place of the full library.

Gold buys the ad-free view, long term charts and reports, the Oracle food suggestions, fasting, custom biometrics and the recipe importer ([Cronometer support](https://support.cronometer.com/hc/en-us/articles/360028026971-Subscription-Types), updated 17 October 2026).

MacroFactor does not do free. Its App Store listing is unusually blunt about it: the app "has a free trial, but does not offer a free subscription tier", and the same listing describes a seven day trial ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/macrofactor-macro-tracker/id1553503471)).

## How many nutrients does each one track?

**Cronometer says up to 95. MacroFactor's help centre documents 56, and I counted them one page at a time.**

MacroFactor publishes a Nutrients collection with one reference article per nutrient it tracks. On 24 October 2026 that collection held 56 articles: 9 on calories and the macro breakdown, 11 amino acids, 9 kinds of fat, 12 vitamins, 10 minerals, and 5 others covering cholesterol, caffeine, alcohol, water and choline ([MacroFactor](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/collections/37-nutrients)).

Cronometer's Track Food page promises "up to 95 nutrients and compounds" ([Cronometer](https://cronometer.com/features/track-food.html)), and its free tier page repeats the 95. Its own home page says 84 in the paragraph about Cronometer Pro, so their marketing does not agree with itself. Either way there is no list I could count, so 95 is their figure and 56 is mine.

![Stacked bar of MacroFactor's 56 documented nutrients split into 9 calorie and macro pages, 11 amino acids, 9 fats, 12 vitamins, 10 minerals and 5 others, against a marker at Cronometer's stated 95](/blog/img/macrofactor-vs-cronometer-2.webp)

Both warn you that the number is a ceiling, not what you will actually see. MacroFactor says most branded products do not report deep micronutrient data, so anything beyond calories, macros and "some major micronutrients" will be undercounted unless you log common foods ([MacroFactor](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/101-view-your-micronutrient-intake-and-more-detailed-information-about-your-macronutrient-intake)).

Cronometer hides some nutrients by default for the same reason, and warns that tracked values "may be significantly lower than they actually are" ([Cronometer support](https://support.cronometer.com/hc/en-us/articles/360000329986-Are-there-nutrients-missing-from-Cronometer), updated 14 August 2026).

So the real question is how many foods in each database carry a full nutrient profile. MacroFactor says 26,500 of roughly 1,360,000 searchable items are micronutrient complete, which is 1.9% ([MacroFactor](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/46-food-search-database)). Cronometer's lab analysed core is the NCC database at over 17,000 entries with data on 70 nutrients, plus USDA SR28 at over 8,000 ([Cronometer support](https://support.cronometer.com/hc/en-us/articles/360018239472-Data-Sources), updated 21 October 2026). Its whole database runs past a million entries and it does not say how much of that is lab analysed ([Cronometer](https://cronometer.com/features/free-nutrition-tracking-app.html)).

![Bar chart of foods with a full nutrient profile: MacroFactor 26,500 micronutrient complete items, Cronometer NCC database over 17,000 and USDA SR28 over 8,000, with MacroFactor's full search index at 1.36 million noted](/blog/img/macrofactor-vs-cronometer-3.webp)

Cronometer does this part better. It also sells a Pro tier aimed at dietitians and research teams, which is not a market MacroFactor is chasing ([Cronometer support](https://support.cronometer.com/hc/en-us/articles/360028026971-Subscription-Types)).

## Which one gets your food into Apple Health?

**Both write. Only MacroFactor reads nutrition back in.**

MacroFactor's integrations page spells out a priority order: manual entry, then its own food log, then Apple Health. A MacroFactor food entry for a day blocks the Health import for that day ([MacroFactor](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/102-integrations)).

Cronometer's Apple Health article covers activity, exercise, biometrics and sleep coming in, and tells you to pick read and write permissions, but nothing in it describes importing food logged elsewhere ([Cronometer support](https://support.cronometer.com/hc/en-us/articles/360020734212-Apple-Health-Apple-Watch), updated 22 October 2026).

One thing changed while I was writing. When I read that article on 20 October 2026 for [our Apple Health comparison](/blog/calorie-apps-apple-health/), it named the nutrient fields Cronometer writes. The 22 October version does not, and no other article in their help centre lists them now. Treat the write list as undocumented until they put it back.

## Which one should you pick?

**Cronometer if you care about micronutrients or want to pay nothing. MacroFactor if you want the coaching algorithm and do not mind the $71.99.**

MacroFactor's pitch is its expenditure algorithm, which reads your weight trend against your logged intake and updates your targets. It needs food logged "at least four times per week, but ideally every day" to keep running ([MacroFactor](https://help.macrofactorapp.com/en/articles/110-how-frequently-do-i-need-to-log-my-nutrition-for-the-expenditure-algorithm-and-weekly-coaching-updates)).

Cronometer's pitch is depth, and it charges for the fast logging paths; [our voice input comparison](/blog/calorie-tracker-voice-input/) has the detail on that.

Mine is the third option and the narrow one. You say what you ate and MealSaid writes calories and macros into Apple Health. If you forget, it calls your phone and asks.

It also has no barcode scanner, no food database to search, no Android app, no web app, no vitamins or minerals at all, and it needs iOS 26. If you want 95 nutrients, Cronometer Basic gives you them for nothing. You can read [how MealSaid works](/blog/how-mealsaid-works/) before deciding.
